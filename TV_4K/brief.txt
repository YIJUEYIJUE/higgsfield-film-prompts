About the project:

4K TV is a hyper-realistic one-minute short built around the simplest possible frame — a guy on a couch flipping channels — that turns a channel-surf into an anthology of five genres in a single sitting. Adil comes home, sinks into the sofa, and every press of the remote opens a new world: a girl on a coral-reef dragon plunging down a 90° waterfall through a butterfly swarm, a snow leopard picking its way down a Himalayan ridge through a shimmering super-telephoto lens, an armored truck outrunning a blizzard monster that hunts beneath the snow like a land-shark, and an elf-versus-orc battlefield where the camera dives between blades of grass to find two palm-sized warriors dueling at the soldiers' feet. Between the worlds, the film keeps returning to the couch — the mom calling from the kitchen, the mumbled "one sec," the thumb on the CH-UP button — until she finally walks in, ready to scold, looks at the screen, and asks the only question that matters: "Wait… is that 4K?"

The film runs on a fully generative pipeline rather than a traditional studio, but treats AI as a directed craft instead of a slot machine. Consistent assets — Adil's likeness in a Y2K outfit, his mustached mom, the teenager's living room, and a fully specced "higgs 4K" remote down to its button layout — are built first in Soul Cinema, GPT Image 2 and Nano Banana Pro, always on grey backgrounds with multiple views, and saved as Elements so nothing drifts across shots. Claude, loaded with a custom prompt-writer skill, translates plain-English direction into structured Seedance 2.0 4K prompts that lock a shared cinematic look: locked-off and FPV camera grammar per world, in-camera speed ramps, screen-in-screen realism with glass sheen and moiré, scale locks and cast locks written straight into the prompt, and diegetic sound only. The real method is iteration — batch after batch generated in 4K, wrong-button presses fixed with a red arrow drawn on the prop sheet, clone armies fixed with a four-elf variety sheet, and finished shots fed back in as video references playing inside the TV, replacing what used to take VFX. The result is studio-scale, big-budget-looking work — photoreal fur, a hundred-warrior battle that holds its geometry, a truck flip where every wheel stays mounted — produced by one person without a crew, render farm or rotoscoping.

Style Prefix
Style: 8K cinematic, photorealistic — no 3D render, no game engine, no game-cutscene aesthetic. Cinematography: naturalistic master cinematography, one camera grammar per world — handheld operator breath in the living room, chase-cam behind the dragon, locked-off super-telephoto for wildlife, weighted aerial-to-ground beats for the chase, gliding FPV for the battle. Lighting: natural light only — window key, contre-jour backlight, camera on shadow side, atmospheric haze; TV screen glow as the living room's second key, its hue shifting with every channel. Color: 60:30:10 — dominant / secondary / accent, with chartreuse #d1ef17 living only in the remote LED and a flicker of screen glow. Camera: physical cine lens, 180° shutter motion blur. Skin: pore-level realism — vellus hair, asymmetric moles, capillary flush, pore-shadow matching on-set light. Acting: top-tier cinematic — micro-pauses before reactions, precise eye-line, wet living eyes with catch-lights, restrained surprise, never cartoonish. Physics: gravity and inertia respected — mass has real weight, wheels never detach, correct contact shadows. No floating props. Composition: rule of thirds + golden ratio, the head always low and to one side so the screen stays unobstructed. Continuity: characters, props, environment identical across every cut. No identity drift. Technical: 24fps smooth motion. No jitter. Audio: environmental SFX and diegetic dialogue only. No music. Music was created during the post-production stage.


Visual Constraints
Photorealistic — no 3D render, no game engine, no plastic CGI gloss, no eye glow. Hard positive locks in every prompt: the TV stays normal household size, the screen content plays 1:1 and text-free, the mother stays offscreen until her scene, and the exact button under the thumb is specified — with a red arrow on the prop sheet when words aren't enough.
Pre-Production: Assets on Canvas

To keep the project visually consistent throughout, we start by creating every asset we'll reuse across the film. You can see this approach up close in Canvas, where we've laid out our full cast — character sheets, prop sheets, and locations. Every sheet lives on a grey background with multiple views — one clear face, one full body, front and back — because after tons of testing, grey backgrounds and multi-angle references are what stop Seedance from guessing. Each asset began in the model that suits it best: Soul Cinema for characters and locations, GPT Image 2 for edits and composites, Nano Banana Pro when a prop needed real depth and shadow instead of a flat 2D read. Everything is saved as an Element, so prompts written in Claude auto-match their references the moment they're pasted into Higgsfield — and finished clips become assets themselves, fed back in to play inside the on-screen TV.

Production: Folder Structure

The work is organized by scenes, with each folder holding the many iterations we explored for every shot — a system that keeps nine scenes of 4K batches navigable without ever limiting how much we experiment. Fittingly, the final folder is dedicated to our earliest model comparisons — the remote that rendered flat, the mandrill-bright monsters, the clone armies — the tests that taught us which model to reach for, when to swap an asset mid-project, and exactly where 4K stops falling apart so the film never does.
