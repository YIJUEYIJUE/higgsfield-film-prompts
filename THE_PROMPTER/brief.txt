This is the project brief for THE PROMPTER, my AI-native short film.

Logline: An AI researcher generates artificial worlds to harvest genuine human emotion — until his experiment begins to slip beyond his control. A psychological science-fiction film about control, feeling, and who is really writing whom.

Note of intent

The film opens on a theatre stage. A few minutes later, the stage turns out to be a film set. A few minutes after that, the film set turns out to be a generation.

That progression is the film. Theatre, cinema, synthetic image — three ways of making people believe in a world that isn't there, stacked one inside the other, each one revealing the previous as a construction. It is meant as a tribute as much as a passage: the new medium doesn't arrive from nowhere, it inherits a very old craft. THE PROMPTER walks its audience through that lineage in ten minutes, and lets them feel the ground move under each layer.

Underneath sits the question that made me want to make it.

Should this medium simply imitate what cinema already does better, and cheaper?

Generative video only becomes a form of its own when a story could not be told without it — when the tool's own nature is the subject. So THE PROMPTER is a film about generated worlds, made of generated worlds; a film about someone arranging circumstances and waiting to see what is felt, written by someone doing exactly that at his console. The drift of the model, the recasting of a face, the resolution that changes between layers — these are not defects worked around. They are the grammar.

And the second question, the one the film never answers out loud:

Can a synthetic image carry a real emotion?

That is precisely what Pacific Research is trying to find out. Their worlds are fabricated; the feelings those worlds provoke are not, and that is the one thing they cannot generate. The film applies its own rule to itself. Every frame you see was prompted. Whether anything was felt is not up to me.

One last thing about the title. THE PROMPTER isn't only the man at the console.
It's me, and everyone else working in this new medium right now — we type circumstances into a machine and wait to see what comes back. We arrange theweather, the faces, the light, and then something arrives that we didn't quite write. The film gives that position a name and follows it one layer up, to ask the only question worth asking: if we are the ones prompting, who is prompting us?

The film

An old theatre. Rebecca loses her line; from the shadows, a prompter feeds her the words. It's the first minute of the film, and nobody notices what has just been established: someone dictates, someone executes.

Everything that follows is a variation on that. THE PROMPTER is a nested-reality story built on a single rule, the same at every level: you may prompt the circumstances, never the emotion. What is felt is the one thing that cannot be generated — which is exactly why someone is collecting it.

Eleven minutes, told in nested layers — the last of them arrives in a single unbroken camera movement.

The Tools

The entire film is generated in Higgsfield Cinema Studio, with Seedance 2.5 for all video and speech. Character sheets: Soul 2.0. Edits, reverse angles and derivations: Nano Banana Pro and Seedream 5. Period exteriors: Soul Cinema. Stubborn view changes: GPT Image 2 — each model chosen per task after field tests. Prompts: Claude, running the project's own skills (below). Edit, sound layout and colour: DaVinci Resolve. No visual material is generated outside Higgsfield.

The worlds

The film moves through distinct realities, and each one is a rule, not a backdrop. The film-within-the-film is a 1930s period picture: theatrical light, painted depth. The world around it is 1960s Los Angeles at night: wet asphalt, warm sconces, brass and oxblood — and nothing in frame newer than the period, because left alone the model drags every shot toward today. Pacific Research is glass, white coves and clean lines. One number recurs across every layer — a room, a door, a session — and one material runs through all of them: brass, from the prompter's lamp to the gramophone standing between two neural devices.

Two kinds of strangeness are kept strictly apart. The generated world drifts the way dreams drift — weather on command, a session that resumes into a subtly different light — and every drift is designed, readable, deliberate. It never breaks the way AI video breaks. For the people inside it, it is the only world there is.

Daniel — her co-star and former partner; the man who follows her; the man who reaches for her. His pursuit reads as a threat for half the film before revealing itself as something more helpless.

The two visitors — the money and the mind. A board member who asks what the audience needs asked, and a scientific partner who speaks exactly once in the film

Sound and Music

One sound carries the film: a rapid mechanical clicking, like text being typed somewhere. It has a fixed timbre and a free rhythm, generated once and laid in post — never generated inside a scene. What it means changes as the film goes.

All dialogue and voices are generated with Seedance 2.5, with per-character voice references carried clip to clip; the Prompter's off-frame commands are produced as a separate generation and laid over the scenes in post, so one identical voice runs through the film. Original music is produced with Suno around a single recurring theme declined per scene; the first act is edited on its rises. Nothing plays under the ending.

The Discipline

Every frame is generated. The whole film is built on a versioned asset registry — currently V10 — where every character state, location plate, prop and voice reference has a fixed name and a frozen descriptor. If an asset isn't in the registry, it isn't in a shot.

Production runs on a numbered body of rules distilled from failed generations: fifty-six and counting, each one written after a shot broke without it. Prompts are built on a fifteen-block skeleton and optics on a ten-anchor FOV ladder. Seedance 2.5 accepts up to fifty references in a single pass — thirty images, ten clips, ten audio files — which is less a budget than a discipline: what goes into a shot is decided by the registry, not by what happens to be available.

The Skills

A skill is a playbook of rules that Claude loads and works by. Two skills and a rulebook, plus the registry that binds them.

CINEDANCE — video prompts. 
A prompt-director agent that works in four silent phases. It deconstructs the shot — active references, spatial layout, first frame, gaze lines, camera side — and strips everything that doesn't belong in this exact frame: unused characters, stale tags, scene numbers, wording carried over from the previous shot. It then runs eighteen failure checks before writing a word — could the first frame come up empty, could left and right flip, could the gaze line reverse, could the lens drift to a comfortable middle, could a prop end up in the wrong hand — and every risk it finds becomes an explicit lock inside the prompt. The output is built in a fixed block order: spatial rules before camera style, optics before aesthetics, lighting as a lock rather than decoration. And it delivers the prompt alone — no reasoning, no checklist, nothing the model shouldn't read.

THE ACTING SYSTEM — performance. 
Its axiom: acting is behavior under pressure, never a display of emotion. A character wants something, something interferes, and they act; emotion arises on its own. Every hero carries a master profile — vocal profile, physical habits with their triggers, a named gait, the mask and the exact condition that cracks it — rewritten for each scene into objective, obstacle and tactics, never pasted. The words "sad", "angry", "afraid" appear nowhere in the prompts. Two rules exist because of the model rather than the actor. Performance is written in states, never in transitions: the model films what is, not what becomes, so a character is already mid-throw, mid-pace, mid-argument. And eye life is specified in every single scene — micro-saccades, blink rate tied to state, live catchlights, the gaze reaching its target a beat before the head turns — because dead eyes are the first thing that betrays a generated performance.

THE PRODUCTION RULES — the inheritance. 
THE PROMPTER is built on the rulebook distilled from The Cully Hill Boys, the feature-length AI film that preceded it: the fifteen-block skeleton, the master shot that opens every scene, positions tied to visible landmarks instead of meters, no lens drift inside a shot, dialogue timed to the model's real clock, states instead of transitions.
Its governing idea is that a rule only counts once it carries its own failure condition. Every law has a name, visible proof in the frame, and a line defining what a failed take looks like — "a rage-twisted face = failed take; a beaming smile = failed take; deadpan = failed take". Prohibitions are written as outcomes, never as bans, because "does not fall on his back" makes the model think about his back. And iteration is surgical: one line changes at a time, the rest word for word — and past twenty attempts, you change the shot, not the sentence. Every shot ever rescued was rescued by changing the shot.

What that film found on the way, this one had waiting on day one — and has extended by twenty rules of its own.

Geography

Every scene opens on a master shot — a wide of about one second, blocking fixed, no lines — so the model photographs the arrangement before anything moves. It is almost always cut in the edit; its job is to establish the geography, not to be seen. Under it sits the spatial map: the canonical geometry of the set, read off the plates once and pasted into every shot of the scene. It names the camera side, the 180° line that is never crossed, and every body tied to a landmark the model can see — never to a measurement, because two metres from the door means nothing to a generator while between the armchair and the glass means everything. And every location reference carries the same explicit line: it controls geometry, materials, light and atmosphere only, never framing. Without it, the model returns a near-copy of the plate instead of using it as a set. This is the observation room, as it is written in the registry:

The camera is on the room side, the door end behind it. The leather ARMCHAIRS
and the low table at FRAME-LEFT against the dark panelled wall; the GLAZED WALL
runs down the RIGHT of the frame; BRIAN'S DESK against the glass at
frame-centre-right, long side parallel to the glass. Beyond the glass, room 307
in frontal symmetry: two pale couches, the rug-and-gramophone island between
them, oxblood curtains framing the server bay at the back.

The consequence is built into the set itself: from Brian's desk, the screen and the two sleeping bodies sit in the same eyeline. The measure and the measured in one frame — no line has to say it.

Acting on the page

What "behavior, not emotion" looks like in practice. This is the corridor scene, act one — Rebecca at a hotel-room door, Daniel calling from the far end. The direction reads: her hand misses the lock on his first call; she tries again, faster; she does not turn. Not one adjective of feeling anywhere in the block. The recognition is physical — the audience understands she knows that voice because her hand fails, two seconds before she says his name. And the dialogue carries the history in four words: "You always say please" replaces a page of backstory, because it says they have had this scene a hundred times. The beat closes on the only physical contact of the scene: the flat of her free hand against his chest, one short contained shove, and he gives ground by a step, hands down. Dialogue is written to the model's real clock — roughly four words a second, plus the written silences, plus one second of clean tail — because a clip that runs long gets filled with invented words.

Continuity

Continuity travels by reference, not by extension. A new generation reads the previous clip only for the state of the scene — positions, facing, wetness, breathing, weather, light — and rebuilds every frame from the registered references. State is preserved; image degradation is not inherited.

And when a shot refuses to come, the answer is never to rephrase it. One linechanges at a time, the rest word for word; past twenty attempts, the shot itself is simplified — split in two, one action removed, the difficulty isolated. Every shot ever rescued was rescued by changing the shot, not the sentence.

Creative Note

The audience is watching a generated film about characters whose reality is generated — and the film's vocabulary is the vocabulary of the tool that makes it: prompt, session, drift, stop. The medium's limitations are not hidden; they are the drama. When the generation drifts in the film, it drifts the way the real tool drifts — except here, the drift is directed.

It is also a love letter to cinema's oldest trick: making us believe in worlds that do not exist. Satoshi Kon is the closest reference — fiction, memory and reality exchanged through a cut, a doorway, a change of space. Here, every door does that.
